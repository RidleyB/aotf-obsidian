In the elder dark, before any hearth remembered fire and before any child knew the comfort of a lamp, there was a black field beneath an empty sky. Upon that field lived the small folk of the earth: mice in their burrows, hares in their warrens, beetles beneath stones, and all the soft and trembling things that feared the talon, the fang, and the cold. 

One night, three great beasts descended from beyond the sky. 

The first was the Black Ox, vast as a mountain, with eyes like drowned stars. Wherever his hooves fell, the earth split open, and from the wounds came deep rivers of bitter blood. He said, “All shall rest beneath me. All shall be still. All shall be mine.” 

The second was the Red Wolf, lean and smiling, with a tongue of honey and teeth hidden behind it. He said, “All shall hunger with me. All shall grow strong by feeding. All shall live forever, so long as they never cease to devour.” 

The third was the Silver Hawk, bright, winged, and terrible, whose feathers shone like knives in moonlight. He said, “All shall look upward. All shall gather beneath my wings. All shall be saved by my light.” 

**Then the three beasts looked upon one another, and each hated what he saw:**
The Ox hated the Wolf, for the Wolf would not lie still. 
The Wolf hated the Hawk, for the Hawk watched all things from above. 
The Hawk hated the Ox, for the Ox would bury the world in his shadow. 

And so, they made war. 

The Black Ox gored the land until valleys became wounds and mountains became broken ribs. The Red Wolf ran laughing through the fields, teaching hunger to every mouth and fear to every heart. The Silver Hawk beat his wings and cast spears of pale fire, burning the dark and all who stood within it. 

The small folk cried out, but the great beasts did not hear them. Or if they did, they mistook the cries for praise. 

For seven nights and seven nights again, the beasts tore at one another. The fields became ash. The rivers turned black. The trees bent away from heaven. The burrows filled with poison. The warrens filled with bones. 

At last, the Silver Hawk struck the Black Ox through the heart. 

The Ox fell so heavily that the world sank beneath him. His body became the floor of all things. His ribs became caverns. His veins became deep roads. His blood seeped downward and upward and inward, until even the roots drank of him. 

And though the Ox was dead, his death did not end. For a dead god is still a heavy thing. Where he rotted, the earth sickened. Where his blood pooled, beasts were born wrong. Where his breath lingered, children dreamed of drowning in blackness. Thus the small folk learned that some corpses do not sleep quietly. 

Then, the Silver Hawk turned upon the Red Wolf and tore him open from throat to belly. “Now I alone remain,” cried the Hawk. “Now the world is mine.” 

But the Wolf, who had always been cleverer than his wounds, did not die as beasts die. His body fell, but his hunger fled into the small folk. It slipped behind the eyes of mice. It curled beneath the tongues of hares. It nested in the hearts of beetles, foxes, crows, and men. And whenever one creature envied another’s warmth, the Wolf whispered, “Feed, and you shall never perish.” Whenever one creature feared the grave, the Wolf whispered, “Take, and you shall never be taken.” Whenever one creature found power within reach, the Wolf whispered, “Bite.” So the Red Wolf lived without a body, and was fed by a thousand mouths. 

**The Silver Hawk saw this and was troubled, for though he had won the war, the world below him was broken:**
The Ox’s corpse poisoned the earth. The
Wolf’s hunger poisoned the heart. 
The small folk shivered in the dark, dying by inches. 

Then the Hawk spread his silver wings and made a lantern from his own radiance. “Come beneath my light,” he called. “Without me, the black rot will claim you. Without me, the Wolf will lead you into one another’s throats. Come beneath my light, and live.” And the small folk came. How could they not? For the dark was full of poison, and the burrows were full of whispers, and the dead Ox dreamed beneath their feet. The Hawk gave them light, and by that light they survived. 

But each night, when they gathered beneath his shining wings, the Hawk grew larger. Each prayer sharpened his talons. Each bowed head brightened his crown. Each life saved became a thread tied about the throats of the living. The small folk told themselves, “The Hawk protects us.” And this was true. They told themselves, “The Hawk is merciful.” And this was sometimes true. They told themselves, “The Hawk asks only that we live beneath his light.” And this was the first lie every cage teaches. 

**So remember this, little ones:** 
Beware the beast that buries you. Beware the beast that hungers through you. Beware most of all the beast that saves you, then names your survival obedience. 

For the Ox is dead and still corrupts the world. 
The Wolf is slain and still feeds. 
The Hawk is victorious and still unsatisfied. 

And beneath the dark field, beneath every burrow, beneath every road the small folk walk, the old war is not ended. It has only grown quiet enough to be mistaken for peace.
